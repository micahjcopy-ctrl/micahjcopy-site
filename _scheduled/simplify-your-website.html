Cut before you add: the website fix that beats a redesign.

Your site got longer every time someone had an idea. A section for the awards, a section for the process, a second testimonial block, a video nobody plays. It performs worse than it did two years ago, and the only fix anyone has offered you is to rebuild the whole thing.

EVERY EXTRA ELEMENT IS FRICTION ASK WHAT TO REMOVE LENGTH FOLLOWS READINESS

The instinct when a site underperforms is to add: more explanation, more proof, more reasons to trust you. I do the opposite. Every element that doesn't help the visitor take the next step is friction, and most sites I look at are carrying years of it. Simplifying your website to increase conversions means asking what can be removed or rewritten, not what to add. Only once that's done can you answer how long the page should be, because that depends on what you sell and how ready the visitor is when they arrive.

How the page got heavy

Nobody sets out to build a cluttered site. Each piece was added for a reason that made sense on its own. A customer asked about the process, so a process section went in. A competitor had a video, so a video went in. Someone worried the page didn't explain enough, so a block of explanation went in. No single addition is the mistake. The mistake is the belief underneath all of them: that more claims, more promises and more detail add up to a stronger case. They add up to a wall, and walls of copy confuse people and bounce them, on a phone most of all.

On one page I studied, only 12 percent of visitors ever reached the first call to action. The cause was dense blocks of jargon-filled explanation sitting between the headline and the button. If a page reads like a textbook, people don't study it. They leave. And the damage isn't only lost attention. A long, visually complex page implies the thing you're selling is complicated too. Promise a simple process on a page that takes effort to read, and visitors feel the contradiction before they can name it, and what they feel is distrust. So the fix is a lighter page, not a new one, and the question is what stays.

The reframe

The question is never what can I add. It's what can be removed or rewritten. Every element either helps the visitor take the next step or it's in the way. Cut the friction. Keep the belief.

How to simplify a website to increase conversions

Walk the page top to bottom and put every block through three questions. Does it help a stranger decide this is for them? Does it make the promise more believable? Does it move them to the next step? A block that does none of those gets cut. A block that's trying to do one of them and failing gets rewritten, not deleted. That distinction matters later.

Start at the top, because that's where the heaviest, least useful things usually live: an oversized logo, a menu with eight options, an About link that invites people off the sales path before they've seen the offer. I go through what belongs above the fold separately; the short version is a promise, proof and a next step, nothing else. Then look for the early call to action that asks someone to book or buy before they know what you do. It pulls people before they're engaged, and a visitor who doesn't know you yet uses it to find out what you cost, then leaves. Then the duplicates: the second testimonial row, the urgency banner with no reason behind it, the paragraph that restates the headline.

This feels like it should make the page worse, and the opposite happens. In one case I studied, a stripped-down version of a page beat the polished one it replaced, and the reason was plain: it was more direct and less mentally taxing. The newer, better-looking version had added complexity, and complexity is friction. Looks weren't the lever, which is a pattern I've written about in why a good-looking site still doesn't convert. Give the lighter version a fair run of traffic before you judge it, but expect it to hold. There is, though, a way to get this wrong in the other direction, and it's the reason I said rewrite rather than delete.

When cutting goes too far

Cutting removes what doesn't help the next step. Gutting removes the belief the page was building, and the symptoms are different. If people click through and leave fast, the page is heavy or confusing, and you cut more. If people take the small step, the enquiry or the opt-in, but not the real one, the page is too light. It isn't building enough belief for the decision you're asking for. The answer there is to refine the copy, not shorten it further: the block about why your approach is different stays, and it gets rewritten in the words your customer would use instead of yours.

So the discipline has two halves. Remove everything that doesn't help the next step, then make sure what's left builds enough belief for that step to feel safe. How much belief that takes is the part most advice skips, because it depends on who's arriving and what you sell.

How long the page should be

Two things set it. The first is how obvious your offer is. Some offers are self-evident: the right person knows instantly it's for them, the way a gym owner knows a service for gym owners is for them, and a short, direct page is all they need. Others are evaluative: the problem has several possible solutions and the visitor is comparing. Someone with a sprained ankle could buy a brace, a spray or a course of physical therapy. A page for an evaluative offer has to acknowledge the alternatives and show why yours is the one, and that takes room. The goal is to make an evaluative offer feel self-evident, so the visitor can decide in a few seconds that this is for them, but you can't get there by cutting the explanation that does the convincing.

The second is how ready the visitor is when they land. Someone who typed your service into Google arrived with intent and often needs a shorter path. Someone who tapped an ad on Facebook out of curiosity needs more before they'll act. The same business can need two different lengths for two different doors. And within either audience, the balance of why versus how shifts. A person who has tried and failed before needs to feel understood before any solution lands, so the page leans on the why. A person who already believes change is possible wants the path, so it leans on the how.

That's the full method. Cut what doesn't help the next step, rewrite what's trying to and failing, then check that the page carries enough belief for the offer you sell and the door people came in through. Most of the time this is the fix, and it costs a fraction of a rebuild. It's also why I diagnose before I quote a new website: when the structure is wrong for the buyer, a rebuild is honestly the answer, but far more often the page is carrying weight it doesn't need.

Common questions

Does a shorter website always convert better?

No. Length follows two things: how obvious the offer is and how ready the visitor is. A self-evident offer shown to search traffic can be short. An offer the visitor is weighing against alternatives, reached from a curious ad click, needs more belief-building. Cut friction, not belief.

What should I remove from my website first?

Start above the fold: the oversized logo, the crowded menu, the About link that pulls people off the sales path, and any button that asks for a booking before the visitor knows what you do. Then the jargon blocks and the duplicate sections below it.

How do I know if I've cut too much?

Watch the gap between the small step and the real one. If people enquire or opt in but don't buy or book, the page is too light and isn't building enough belief. Refine the copy that explains why your approach is different. Don't shorten it further.
