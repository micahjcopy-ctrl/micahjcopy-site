When to kill a Meta ad, and when to scale it.

It's nine in the morning. The ad you launched yesterday spent forty dollars with nothing to show, so you turn it off. The other one got two sales overnight, so you triple its budget. By Friday both decisions have cost you money, and the dashboard can't tell you why.

SPEND, NOT DAYS READ THREE WINDOWS ADD WEIGHT SLOWLY

Most of the damage in a Meta ads account isn't done by bad ads. It's done by reasonable decisions made a day too early. Killing on a bad morning and scaling on a lucky night both feel like management, and both throw away the only thing that makes an ad judgeable: enough spend, measured against a target you set before launch. The fix is a short set of rules, written down in advance, that make the nine o'clock decision for you.

An ad earns a verdict with spend, not with days

Time is the wrong unit, and so are impressions. Seven dollars of spend and a hundred and fifty impressions with no clicks isn't a failure. It's nothing yet. A test of an ad's first lines can be read on cost per click after about 1,500 impressions; whether an ad set makes money can't be read that way. What an ad set needs before it can be judged is spend measured against your target cost per acquisition, the number you worked out from your margins and cash flow before you spent a dollar on Meta ads. Two rules follow from that. Set the ad set's daily budget at least equal to the target CPA, so a $100 target gets $100 a day, and run it two or three days, because you need a few CPA cycles before anything means anything. Then let the ad set spend about three times the target CPA before you call it a loser. Part of that patience is attribution: a sale from Tuesday's click can land in Thursday's report, and the ad you killed on Wednesday morning was in the middle of paying off.

One condition sits under both rules, and it's the one people skip. The number you judge against has to be the event you asked Meta to optimize for. If you optimized for leads and you're now judging on purchases, nobody is wrong, you're just reading two different scoreboards, which is the whole point of choosing the right conversion event before launch.

When to turn off a Facebook ad: read three windows first

Once an ad set has that much spend behind it, I don't look at one number. I look at the same number across three windows: the last seven days, the last three, and yesterday. Each window tells part of the story and the decision comes from all three. A dip in the last three days sitting inside a strong two- or four-week history is a hiccup, so hold. A good stretch that only ever showed up briefly, inside a weak overall story, is a loser that got lucky, so cut. To keep myself honest I set green, yellow and red zones around the target, the same zones I draw when I do the math before launch: green gets more budget, yellow holds, red gets reduced or cut.

Reduced or cut, and the difference is history. A new ad set that underperforms fast gets cut, because it has nothing in the bank. A proven performer in a rough patch gets its budget reduced and room to recover, the way you'd treat an employee with a good year and a bad month. Past performance is data too, and a rule that ignores it will kill your best ad on its worst Tuesday.

The rule

An ad earns a decision after it has spent about three times your target CPA, read across the last seven days, the last three and yesterday. Not after a day. Not after a spike.

Scale like adding weight to a bar

The green zone says add budget, and this is where the second mistake lives. Spend is the weight on the bar. An ad with a stronger creative foundation holds more of it before results slow, and any ad fails if you load it all at once. So I add in steps, and the size of the step comes from the gap between your CPA and your target. A $20 CPA against a $50 target has room, so the steps can be bigger. A $42 CPA against the same target is thin, so the steps are five to ten percent. In practice I work between roughly ten and twenty percent every two or three days, smaller as the budget grows and the margin thins, and I move each ad set on its own rather than the whole campaign at once. If a step makes results dip, I take the weight back off and let it recover, instead of dropping the bar.

Two things I don't do while scaling. I don't duplicate the winning ad set into a fresh one to carry the bigger budget, because the copy starts learning from zero while the original keeps the history. A duplicate is for when a setting has to change, and that history is the same reason "learning limited" isn't a reason to rebuild. And I don't edit its live settings, because that restarts the learning too. Raise the budget on the ad set that's working and leave the rest of it alone.

Don't scale on a spike

The hardest green to resist is the one that isn't real. In one account I looked at, two purchases at fifty cents each showed up as a 97 times return on ad spend. Nobody should scale on that, and the reason is sample size. Return on ad spend only means something when there are enough purchases behind it to trust the average order value, and two orders is a coin that landed heads twice. If CPA is steady and ROAS is swinging, you're looking at order-value noise, not performance.

And when a result looks too good, suspect the placement before you believe it. A video campaign I went through showed penny plays and a hook rate over ninety percent on cold traffic, which is a number real feeds don't produce. The placement breakdown showed nearly all the spend had gone to Audience Network rewarded video, where people sit through thirty seconds of your ad to get back to a game. In-feed viewers were hooking at about twenty-three percent, which was the real figure. Check the placement breakdown before you add a dollar, and exclude rewarded video from any audience you build on views, or you'll be retargeting people who never cared.

The ad you're about to kill might be feeding the one you love

The mirror image of the fake winner is the fake loser. Meta serves ads in sequence, and you have to judge them that way. A top-of-funnel video with a weak direct CPA can be the thing warming the people who then convert on your bottom-of-funnel ad. Turn it off because its own column looks bad and the path breaks: the closer keeps running, its results fall, and now you're reaching for the switch on that one too. Before you kill, ask what the ad feeds. Before you scale, ask what fed it.

Which is why the real move here isn't any single rule. It's writing them down before launch, so the nine o'clock decision is made by the rule and not by your nerves. Mine fit on a card:

  • Daily budget at least equal to target CPA, for two or three days, before any read.
  • No verdict on an ad set before it has spent about three times target CPA.
  • Three windows, last seven, last three, yesterday, before any change.
  • Steps of ten to twenty percent, smaller when the margin is thin or the budget is large.
  • Reduce proven performers in a slump. Cut new ad sets that fail fast.
  • When it looks too good, check the placement. When it looks too bad, check the sequence.

Common questions

How long should I let a Facebook ad run before turning it off?

Judge on spend, not days. Give the ad set a daily budget at least equal to your target CPA, run it two or three days, and let it spend about three times the target CPA before you call it. Attribution lags, so a sale from Tuesday's click can show up in Thursday's report.

How fast can I increase the budget on a winning Meta ad?

In steps, on the same ad set, every two or three days. Ten to twenty percent is my working range, dropping to five to ten when the CPA sits close to target or the budget is already large. If results dip after a step, reduce and let it recover rather than cutting.

Should I turn off an ad with a high CPA if the campaign is profitable overall?

Not without checking what it feeds. Meta serves ads in sequence, and a top-of-funnel ad with a poor direct CPA may be warming the people who convert on another ad. Look at the campaign's results as a connected path before you pull one piece out of it.
