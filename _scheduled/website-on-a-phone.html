Website & Conversion · Mobile and speed

Your website gets judged on a phone, in a hurry, on a bad connection.

You approved the design on a big monitor over office wifi. Your customer meets it on a four-year-old phone in a parking lot, with one bar of signal and a thumb. Those are two different websites, and only one of them makes you money.

APPROVE IT ON A PHONE SECONDS, NOT SCORES A BROKEN BUTTON ENDS IT

A slow website is losing customers before they read a word, and a shrunk-down desktop layout loses the ones who waited. Neither looks like a design problem, because on your screen the site is fine. It shows up as traffic that never becomes a lead. The fix starts with two numbers that tell you whether anyone is seeing your pitch at all: how many ad clicks actually become page views, and how many real seconds the page takes to load on a phone.

The site you approved isn't the site they see

For most businesses I look at, the phone is where the traffic is, and the attention is there too. In one set of session data I studied, mobile visitors were scrolling more than twice as deep as desktop visitors. So the version you sign off on, the big one with the wide hero image and the three columns, is the version the fewest customers ever meet. Sign-off happens on the desktop for an understandable reason: that's where the builder works and where the mockup looks best. It's also the view that tells you least.

And the phone they meet it on is older than yours. If your customers skew older or less technical, some of them are on phones several generations back, and a clean, modern page that loads fine on a new device can render wrong or stall on theirs. Many of them arrive through the browser inside the Facebook or Instagram app, which can block forms and break embedded video. The right starting point for any check is the device that drives the most traffic, which is almost never the one on your desk. And the first thing that goes wrong on that device happens before a single word appears.

How a slow website loses customers you've already paid for

The number I check first on any site that's running ads is this: of every hundred people who click, how many actually land on the page? My working rule is that around 85 landing is healthy. When it's 50 or 60, something is eating the gap, and usually it's load time. The page took long enough that a person who had already decided to click gave up mid-load. You paid for every one of those clicks. The ad did its job, and the page never showed up for work. That's the pattern behind why your ads get clicks and nobody buys: the money goes missing on the page, not in the ad account.

Check real seconds, not a score. A speed-test grade tells you what a robot thinks. A stopwatch on a phone over cellular tells you what a customer feels. Load the page with wifi off and count. Past about three seconds, bounce climbs sharply and paid traffic gets wasted, and the usual cause is boring: oversized, uncompressed photos. The full-width hero shot that was approved on the big monitor is often the heaviest thing on the page. Sites built on some of the popular drag-and-drop builders are often slow out of the box as well. Compressing and resizing images is unglamorous, and it's the single fix that most often closes the click-to-view gap. Then, once the page has loaded, the second judgment starts.

The two checks

Clicks that become page views, and real seconds on a phone over cellular. If either number is bad, nothing else on the page matters yet, because nobody is seeing it. Approve designs on a phone, not a monitor.

Once it loads, the layout has to work with a thumb

A responsive site reflows to fit a small screen. That isn't the same as being designed for one. A shrunk-down desktop layout puts the key graphic at a size that needs pinch-zooming, and if a visitor has to pinch to read the thing that explains your offer, that's friction, and most of them won't. On a phone most visitors barely get past the top, so what sits in that first screen is most of what they'll ever see. The graphic has to be rebuilt for the phone: stacked images, bigger type, icons with steps, instead of a smaller copy of the desktop version. The headline has to fit without wrapping into a paragraph. The button has to be in the first couple of scrolls and short enough to read at a glance.

And it has to work. A broken button on a phone tanks a page, and in my experience the reason is usually that nobody tapped it on a real phone before launch. The signals are easy to read in a free session-recording tool: rage clicks, where someone taps the same spot over and over, mean a button isn't responding; dead clicks mean something looks tappable and isn't. If an ad is getting clicks and the page is getting views but almost nobody is converting, suspect a technical fault before you suspect the copy. Nothing deeper is worth optimizing while the button doesn't work or the page is slow. The argument a page makes, every section setting up the next, only matters once it can be read; I lay that out in a good website reads like one argument. The phone decides whether anyone gets to read it.

What to ask for before you sign off

None of this needs a developer to check. Before you approve a new site or a redesign, ask the builder for a phone-first preview, and look at it on your phone, not in a browser window squeezed narrow. Load it over cellular and time it in seconds. Load it on the oldest phone you can borrow. Tap every button and fill in the form on the phone. Ask what the images weigh, and ask for the click-to-page-view number once ads are running. A builder who can't answer those, or who shows you the desktop mockup first, is building for the monitor. Once the site is live, a free session-recording tool shows you the rest: very short active time on a page points at load speed or a mismatch with the ad that sent them, and the mobile scroll map shows exactly where thumbs stop.

If your current site fails these, it's one of the cases where I'll say a rebuild is the right call, and I say that rarely, as I explained in do you need a new website or a better one. Most problems are one element. A site that doesn't load, or doesn't work on the phone in your customer's hand, isn't one element. It's the whole pitch failing to arrive.

Common questions

How fast should my website load on a phone?

Time it on a phone over cellular, in real seconds, rather than trusting a speed score. Past about three seconds, bounce climbs and paid clicks get wasted. The usual cause is oversized, uncompressed photos, and compressing them is the fix that most often closes the gap.

Why do my ads get clicks but my page gets far fewer visits?

Compare link clicks to page views. If around 85 of every 100 clicks become views, the page is loading fine. At 50 or 60, the page is taking too long and people are leaving mid-load. You've paid for those clicks. The fix is usually load time, starting with image size.

Is a responsive website enough?

No. Responsive means the layout reflows to a small screen. It doesn't mean the key graphic is readable without pinch-zooming, the button sits in the first scrolls, or every tap works on an older phone. Check those on a real device before you sign off.
