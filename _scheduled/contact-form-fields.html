Every form field costs you customers.

People get to your contact page, or your booking page, and then nothing. The traffic is there. The form is where it stops. And the form is usually the part of the site nobody thought about, because "contact us" felt like the easy bit.

EVERY FIELD IS A TOLL KEEP WHAT PAYS SAY WHAT HAPPENS NEXT

Pull up your contact form and count the fields. Each one is a toll you're charging a person who was about to raise their hand, and most forms charge far more than the lead is willing to pay. Then the button at the bottom asks for something instead of offering something, and the page after the click says nothing about what happens next. How many fields should a contact form have? Only the ones that pay for themselves, and the way to tell which those are is to understand what each one costs.

What a field actually costs

Fields look free. They aren't. In one case I studied, a funnel aimed at high-income buyers underperformed, and the fix that turned it around was removing the phone-number field. Conversions jumped. In another, a checkout for a digital product was asking for a shipping address, for something that would never ship. Dropping those fields took completion from about a third of people to nearly half. In a third, an application form produced 22 applications and 7 booked calls, and the drop-off showed where it broke: about a fifth of applicants quit at the final step, the plain name, email and phone box. My working rule from that one is simple. If fewer than half the people who start an application make it to the call, the form is broken.

The mechanism behind all three is the same. Filling in a form creates an expectation of progression: each answer feels like a step toward something. When the next field asks for information the person can't see a reason for, or the last step is a flat demand for contact details, the momentum dies. They were saying yes, and the form asked them to prove it first. So every field has to answer the same question the rest of the page does: does this help the visitor take the next step? Some fields do earn their place, and you need a way to tell which.

How many fields should a contact form have?

As few as pay for themselves. A field pays for itself in one of two ways. Either the answer changes what happens next, because you route the lead differently or tailor the call, or the small effort of answering it filters for the kind of lead you want. Everything else is a toll with no road behind it. My rule for most service businesses is a name, one way to reach them, and at most one question that shapes the conversation. If you can't say what you'd do differently with the answer, cut the field.

The second kind of field is real, though, and it's where the counter-case lives. In another case I studied, an application form sat after the purchase, before the buyer got access, and it attracted higher-quality leads who went on to buy the expensive thing. The buyers were affluent and short on time, and they rewarded a process that tailored itself to them. The form earned its place because it came after the first yes, not before it. That's the sequence: get the small commitment with almost no friction, then ask the qualifying questions once the person has a reason to answer them.

If you need the questions, split the form instead of lengthening it. One booking form with everything on it feels like overwhelm at the gate. Three steps feel like progress: a simple booking form, then a separate questionnaire, then a short page that reframes the offer and shows a result or two. Each step builds investment in the next. And frame every question so it builds anticipation for the call: "we ask this so we can tailor the call to your situation" turns a demand into a service. All of this assumes the page above the form has already told the visitor what you do and who it's for. If it hasn't, no form fixes that, and I cover it in why your website isn't getting leads. The form is the last step of the argument, which is why the words on the button matter as much as the fields above it.

The rule

Every field is a toll. Keep the ones that pay for themselves: the answer changes what you do next, or the effort filters for the lead you want. Everything else goes, or moves to after the first yes.

The button should offer, not ask

Read your button out loud. "Submit." "Contact us." "Pay $20." "Complete your order." Every one of those asks the visitor to give something. The buttons that convert offer something instead: "Reserve your spot" in place of "Pay $20," "Complete your registration" in place of "Complete your order," so the buyer feels like they're starting something rather than finishing it. The principle is the same one that makes a framed question work: the words put the visitor's next step in terms of what they get.

Match the button to how much the visitor knows. For someone who already understands what you do, "Book your call" is right, and it should be visible without hunting, because small friction like a hidden link or an unclear menu kills the click. For someone who doesn't know you yet, an early "Book a call" makes them click to check the price, and they filter you on cost before they've seen the value. Give that visitor a smaller step first: "See how it works." And whatever the button says, it has to work on a phone, which is the check I walk through in your website gets judged on a phone. The button is a promise about what the click leads to. The page after it has to keep that promise.

Tell them what happens after they click

The button made a promise. The close is where many pages break it, and they break it quietly, because the owner never sees the page a lead sees after they submit. Uncertainty at that moment is what loses the lead you'd just won. So say exactly what happens next, in order. For a purchase: ships within 24 hours, with tracking. For a call: you fill in this form, I review it, you get a confirmation with the details, and on the call we cover these three things. Write it before the button and again on the page after it. The visitor who knows what's coming shows up.

If the next step is a calendar, look at it the way a stranger does. A link to a wide-open calendar with every slot free says you're not busy, and it costs credibility at the exact moment you need it. Put the calendar on a branded page with a headline that names what the call is, and limit the available slots to a couple of days out. It reads as demand and it improves show rates. Then mind the gap between the booking and the call, because people drift in the wait. A reminder with a result from someone like them keeps the decision warm. That gap is the start of the follow-up problem, and most forms hand the lead straight into it.

That's the whole fix, in the order a lead experiences it. Cut the form to the fields that pay. Move the qualifying questions to after the first yes. Rewrite the button so it offers. Say what happens next, and make the calendar look like a business people book. None of it needs a redesign, and it sits exactly where the leads were vanishing.

Common questions

How many fields should a contact form have?

As few as pay for themselves: a name, one way to reach the person, and at most one question that changes how you handle the lead. If you can't say what you'd do differently with an answer, cut the field. Qualifying questions belong after the first yes, on a second step.

Should I ask for a phone number on a contact form?

Only if you'll actually call, and even then test it. In one case I studied, removing the phone-number field was the single change that turned an underperforming funnel around. If you need the number to deliver the call, ask for it on the booking step, with a line saying why.

What should the button on my contact form say?

Something that offers rather than asks: Reserve your spot, See how it works, Get my plan, rather than Submit or Contact us. Match it to how much the visitor knows: a direct booking button for people who understand your offer, a smaller step for people who don't yet.
