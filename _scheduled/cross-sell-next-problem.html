Email & SMS · Cross-sell emails

Your next product is your customer's next problem.

Your cross-sell email shows a row of things other people bought. It's generated by what sold together, not by what this customer needs now, and that's why it sits there unclicked. The product they'll actually buy next is the answer to the problem that shows up once the first one is solved.

ALSO-BOUGHT IS A GUESS BEFORE, DURING, AFTER SOLVE THE NEXT PROBLEM

Someone bought your sleep support a month ago. What are they going to ask you next? Not what other people bought; they don't know those people. A month in, sleeping better, the question in their head is why they still hit a wall at three in the afternoon. That's the next problem, and whoever solves it gets the next order. "Customers also bought" answers a question nobody asked. The next product is the next problem on the path, and your cross-sell email should name it.

Why "customers also bought" doesn't move

The also-bought block is built on co-purchase: whatever landed in the same carts as the thing this person bought. That's a pattern across strangers, and it's silent about the one thing that matters, which is where this customer is now. The sale moved them. Before they bought, they had the problem they came with. During, they have the friction of actually using it. After it works, a new set of problems exists that didn't before, because solving the first one exposed them. The also-bought block talks to the person they were on the day they checked out. The next order comes from talking to the person they are now.

Your lineup has to do the same job. A good catalog doesn't guess at random add-ons. It maps the sequence of problems along the customer's path, so the next product is the obvious next step and the pitch is "you solved this, here's what comes after it." That's a different sentence from "you might also like," and customers can tell the difference from the subject line. It's also the classic retail model: acquire a customer once, then keep selling them the next relevant thing for years. The also-bought block is that model with the "relevant" taken out.

The rule

The next product is the next problem. Map what shows up before, during and after someone uses what you sell, and the cross-sell writes itself as "you solved this, here's what comes next."

A cross sell email strategy built on the sequence of problems

Those three places are the map. Take your main product and write them as three columns. Before: the problems that bring people to it in the first place. During: what gets in the way while they use it. After: what's left, or newly visible, once it works. Every entry is a legitimate problem and a candidate product, or a referral to someone you trust if you can't build it. The point is to support the whole transformation instead of one step of it. The during column rarely becomes a product, and it still earns its place: it's the list of things your consumption emails have to handle before anyone reaches the after column at all.

Sleep support runs like this. Before: wired at night, a mind that won't stop. During: remembering to take it, what to pair it with, how long before it kicks in. After: sleeping well and noticing the afternoon slump, because once nights are fixed, energy is the next thing the body complains about. Someone who bought the sleep product will ask about energy. Energy is the next product in line, and it gets offered as the sequel, not as a stranger's cart. The same map works outside supplements: a weight-loss catalog runs from eating habits before, to cravings during, to loose skin after, and each of those is a product.

Not every problem on the map is ready to be sold. Filter by what buyers already know they need and already ask about. The obvious adjacent problem, the one that shows up in support tickets and community questions, is an easy next sale. The abstract one that needs explaining first belongs later in the catalog, once trust is deeper. Your tickets are the map. Read them for the question that keeps appearing a few weeks after delivery. That's your after column, already written in the customer's own words.

One product? Diversify what it does.

If you sell one thing, the map doesn't change. The product column does. Instead of a second item, the answer to the after problem is the same product aimed at it, which means showing that one product solves problems A, B, C, D and E instead of only the one it was launched for. The sleep product that helps with jet lag, with recovery, with shift work, with the first week of a new routine. Each of those is a cross-sell email with nothing new to ship: same product, next problem. A small reframe to serve a parallel need reaches buyers the original angle never did, and it fills the gap until the second product exists. When the second product does come, it should be the problem your tickets named most.

Where the email goes, and who gets it

The next-problem email has a fixed slot in the post-purchase arc: after the celebration, after the how-to timed to arrival, after the check-in and review ask. Fourth. It lands on someone whose first problem is solved, which is the only moment "what comes after it" makes sense. Send it earlier and you're offering the sequel to someone who hasn't finished the first chapter. The signal that they have is a reply to the check-in or a review, not a date on the calendar, so the slot is a position in the arc rather than a day number. Whether it's a second product or the same one pointed at the next problem, it takes the same slot. The rest of the arc is in the post-purchase flow.

Who it goes to follows from the slot. Inside the flow, it reaches a buyer only once the check-in or the review says the first problem is solved; a first-timer who hasn't replied is still in "use what you have," and the sequel waits. As a campaign, the next-problem email belongs to the repeat-buyer tier, the two-to-five-order people whose job is expansion, which I split out in the buyer tiers. A VIP needs early access, not a nudge toward the next item. And the next-problem email doesn't need a discount. The reason to buy is a problem the customer can feel, and a code on top of it says the product can't carry its own weight, the damage I lay out in why constant sales kill a store. Get the slot and the tier right, and the sequel sells at full price to someone who was already going to look for it. Do that across a lineup and the catalog, not the first sale, carries most of the revenue, because you acquired the customer once and then solved their problems in order.

Mapping the before, during and after for a lineup, and turning the map into the post-purchase cross-sell, is the part of retention work most owners have never had done, because it's half product strategy and half email. Common questions

What's wrong with a "customers also bought" cross-sell email?

It's built on what landed in other people's carts, which says nothing about where this customer is now. After the purchase a new problem exists that didn't before. A cross-sell that names that next problem gets the order. A row of unrelated products gets ignored.

How do I decide which product to cross-sell?

Map your main product's before, during and after. Each problem in the after column is a candidate. Pick the one buyers already ask about in support tickets and community questions; the obvious adjacent problem is an easy next sale. Save the abstract ones for later, once trust is deeper.

When should the cross-sell email go out?

Fourth in the post-purchase flow, after the celebration, the how-to timed to arrival, and the check-in with the review ask. It should land on someone whose first problem is solved, because "you solved this, here's what comes next" only works once the first thing has worked.
