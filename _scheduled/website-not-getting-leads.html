Why your website isn't getting leads: the five-second test.

You've got traffic. Analytics says people are showing up. The form stays empty and the phone stays quiet, and what you keep telling yourself is that they'll look around and figure it out. They won't. A stranger gives the top of your page a few seconds, and what it says in those seconds decides everything.

NOBODY LOOKS AROUND FIVE SECONDS, SIX QUESTIONS ONE AUDIENCE, ONE OUTCOME

"They'll look around." That's the sentence I hear from owners whose site gets visitors and no leads, and it's the belief doing the damage. Almost nobody looks around. A stranger lands, glances at the top, and either understands what this is, who it's for and why it matters, or leaves to find a page that tells them. The test for whether your site passes takes five seconds and one person who doesn't know your business, and you can run it before the end of the day.

Why your website isn't generating leads: nobody looks around

You know your business, so your homepage reads clearly to you. Every line on it makes sense because you already know what's behind it. The visitor doesn't. They arrived from a search or an ad with one question in their head, some version of "will this work for me," and they're scanning your page for the answer, not taking the tour. If the answer isn't at the top, they don't go hunting for it on the About page or the third service tab. They go back to the results.

On a phone it's worse. People barely scroll past the first screen. So the whole decision gets made on what fits there: the headline, the line under it, the image, the first button. Everything you put further down, the testimonials, the process, the service descriptions you spent a weekend on, is read by the people the top already convinced. For most visitors the top isn't the introduction to your site. It's the whole site.

I keep coming back to a teardown I studied where a page had a hundred thousand organic views, strong engagement, and essentially zero sales. It wasn't the ad format and it wasn't the topic. The page was a bundle of a book, some merchandise and a community, and nowhere did it say who it was for or what problem it solved. People shared it, and almost nobody bought from it. The fix wasn't more traffic. It was one simple page, one well-defined audience, one product, and a top that said so. Until a page communicates the outcome it delivers, no amount of traffic saves it. Whether yours does is what the test checks.

The five-second test, and the six questions behind it

The test is this. Can a stranger glance at the top of your page and understand what's offered, who it's for, and what they get from it? Five seconds is a glance. I'm not claiming anyone has clocked it to the second, and the number isn't the point. The point is that a visitor's first look is a glance, not a read, and your top either survives a glance or it doesn't.

Behind that glance are six questions every page has to answer, in roughly this order. Who is this for? Why am I here? What am I looking at? Why should I care? Why should I believe this? What do I do next? A stranger works through them in seconds without knowing they're doing it. The top of your page has to answer the first four outright and point plainly at the last two. If it answers one of them, or answers all six vaguely, the visitor is doing your work for you, and visitors don't do your work. They leave. Which raises the question of why so many pages, built by people who know their business cold, fail a test this simple.

The test

Show the top of your homepage, phone-sized, to someone who doesn't know your business. Give them five seconds. Ask what you sell, who it's for, and what they'd click. If they guess, your visitors are guessing too, and guessing visitors leave.

Why pages fail it: they try to say everything

Most homepages don't fail the test by saying too little. They fail it by saying too much. The owner, wanting to miss nobody, promises everybody. A headline that offers weight loss and better sleep and business success doesn't make three kinds of people feel seen. It confuses all three. The way I write a top is the opposite: one audience, one core problem, one solution that's clearly different, one outcome. Every promise you add past that makes the stranger work harder to find the one that's theirs, and the stranger won't work.

The other way to fail is abstraction. Owners describe what they do in the language of their trade, or in brand language that sounds impressive and means nothing from the outside. In one teardown of a tutoring business, the page talked about tutoring. Parents don't want tutoring. They want Ds turned into As in a few weeks, and that sentence belonged at the top. A coding course for finance people talked about "professional applications"; what its audience would have understood was "turn your Excel skills into Python." Same product, but one version is anchored to something the reader already knows. Every point of abstraction is a point where someone drops off. Say what it does, for whom, in the words they'd use to describe the problem to a friend. That's the fix, and it's the thing you're listening for when you run the test.

Run the test on your site today

Do this before you touch anything. Open your homepage on your phone and take a screenshot without scrolling. That's your top. Send it to one person who doesn't know your business: a friend, a relative, whoever's at the next table. Tell them to look at it for five seconds and then answer three questions. What does this business sell? Who is it for? What would you click? Write their answers down in their words, not yours.

If they get all three right, or close, your top passes, and the leak is lower on the page or later in the follow-up, which is a different problem with a different fix. If they guess, or say "some kind of consulting," or ask you what it is, you've found the leak, and it's the cheapest leak there is. Don't add a section to explain. Rewrite the top: one audience, their problem in their words, what you do about it, one button. Then run the test again with a different stranger. This is also why I tell owners to be careful with redesign quotes. You rarely need a new website to fix a top that fails this test. You need a better first screen, and you can have it by tonight.

The five-second test is the first thing I run on any site that gets traffic and no leads, and most of the time it's where the leak is. If you'd rather have me run it, with the numbers and the recordings behind it, it's the first step of every site I build or fix, before any design happens.

Common questions

Why is my website getting traffic but no leads?

Usually because a stranger can't tell what you sell, who it's for and why it matters from the top of the page. Visitors don't look around for the answer. They glance at the first screen and leave if it isn't there, especially on a phone, where most people barely scroll.

What is the five-second test for a website?

Show the top of your homepage, phone-sized, to someone who doesn't know your business for five seconds, then ask what you sell, who it's for and what they'd click. It's a test, not a measured statistic. If they guess, your visitors are guessing too.

What should the top of my homepage say?

One audience, one problem in their words, one outcome, and one next step. Pages fail by promising everyone everything, or by describing the service in trade language. Every extra promise and every abstract phrase is a place where someone drops off.
