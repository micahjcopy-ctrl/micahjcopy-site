What to email your customers when you're not running a sale.

Look at your revenue graph and it's a row of spikes with flat line between them. Each spike is a promo. Each one is a little shorter than the last, so the next discount goes a little deeper. The problem isn't the promos. It's the flat line, and what you send during it, which is usually nothing.

PROMOS ARE WITHDRAWALS DEPOSIT BETWEEN SALES HELP THEM USE IT

Most owners who only email during sales have a sensible reason: when there's nothing to sell, there's nothing to say. So the list hears from you on promo day and goes quiet until the next one. That silence is where the promos lose their pull. Every email that asks for money is a withdrawal from a relationship, and you can't keep withdrawing from an account you never deposit into. The emails between sales are the deposits. Done right, they're also what earns the second order.

Why each promo pulls a little less

A list learns what your emails mean. If the only email you send carries a discount, your subscribers learn that an email from you is a coupon, and they learn it fast. They stop opening unless the subject line has a number in it. The ones who do open have been trained to wait for the next sale before buying anything at full price. So each promo has to be a little louder to get the same response, and louder means a deeper discount, which is the margin problem I covered in why constant sales quietly kill your store.

Underneath that, a list has finite buying power. Your subscribers only have so many dollars to spend with you in a given month, and a promo spends them all at once. Run promos back to back and you aren't creating demand, you're pulling the same demand forward and paying a discount for the privilege. When the list tires of being asked, it stops responding, and the store starts leaning on new customers to replace the ones who went quiet. That's the trap. A program that only ever asks for money ends up forcing you back into acquisition, which is the expensive problem email was supposed to solve.

In one account I audited, the owner's own read was that revenue only moved when a promo went out. That was right. But the reason wasn't that promos work. It was that a promo was the only email in the account that gave anyone a reason to act that day. Nothing else had a job. So the question isn't whether to send fewer promos. It's what the other emails are for.

The reframe

A non-promo email is a deposit. The sale is the withdrawal. If every email asks for money you're overdrawn, and the list responds the way an overdrawn account does: it stops paying out. Give something between sales and the next sale lands on people who still trust you.

What an email that isn't selling is for

A deposit email has one job: the reader should close it slightly better off than they opened it. Not transformed. Slightly. Shift one belief they've been carrying, or teach them one small thing, or reframe a problem they've been living with. Every open should pay off, and when it does the reader comes back for the next one, because people return to the sources that make them feel good. That's the whole mechanism. Over a few months, a reader who's been made a little more capable by each email is someone who sees your product the way you do, and the next pitch doesn't feel like a pitch. It feels like the obvious next step.

For a customer, as opposed to a subscriber who hasn't bought yet, the highest-value deposit is specific. Help them get more out of what they already bought. A second order comes from a result, and the result comes from using the first order properly, which most customers don't manage on their own. A deposit that gets the product used pays out twice: the reader is better off today, and there's now a reason for them to come back. So the between-sale emails to buyers aren't filler to keep the list warm. They're the emails that decide whether there's a second order at all.

Three emails to send when you have nothing to sell

Three types do most of the work. Say you sell a vitamin C serum.

The first is how to use it better. Not the instructions on the box, the detail that changes the result: morning, on damp skin, under sunscreen, and why that order matters. A customer who reads that and does it tomorrow just got a better result from a bottle they already paid for. That's a deposit they can feel.

The second is what's behind it. Where the ingredient comes from, why you chose this form of it over the cheaper one, what you rejected on the way to the formula. The behind-the-scenes email is the kind people forward, and it does quiet work: it installs the criteria they'll use to judge every competitor from now on, and your product is the one that meets them.

The third is what other customers do with it. One customer's routine, in her own words, including the small thing she does that you'd never have thought to suggest. It's proof and it's a how-to at the same time, and readers see themselves in it and try it. End it by asking them to hit reply and tell you theirs. Replies feed the next version of this email, and they tell inbox providers that people want your mail, which protects every send after it.

None of the three asks for money in the body. If there's a product to mention, it goes in a P.S., one line, tied to what the email just taught. Someone who just learned the damp-skin trick is a reasonable person to tell about the moisturizer that goes on top. That's the pitch finding its place inside the deposit instead of replacing it.

How I rotate it, and where the second order comes from

I run buyer lists on a simple alternation: a relationship week, then a pay-day week. The relationship week is deposits, two or three of the emails above, no ask in the body. The pay-day week carries the offer, and because it follows a week of giving, it lands on people who've been opening. Then back to relationship. Every two or three weeks, one email has no link at all. Nothing to click, just something worth reading. It resets the relationship in a way nothing else does, and it's the email people reply to.

The order matters more than the count. How many sends a week is its own question, and I've written about how often you should actually email your list, but frequency without a job for each email is just more noise. Give, give, give, then ask. The ask is where the second order comes from, and when it arrives it shouldn't be a random discount. It should be the thing their first purchase says they'll need next, which is the logic in your next product is your customer's next problem. A customer who's been getting more from the serum for a month, and who now knows what you chose and why, is ready to hear about what comes after it. You didn't have to discount it. You deposited enough that the withdrawal cleared.

Common questions

Should I stop running sales entirely?

No. Keep promos, but make them the withdrawal rather than the whole program. Run a relationship week of non-pitch emails, then a pay-day week with the offer, and alternate. The promo pulls harder when it follows a week of giving.

What do I email customers who already bought and don't need anything yet?

Help them get more from what they bought: how to use it better, what's behind it, and what other customers do with it. The customer who feels the product work is the one who reorders, so these emails earn the second order instead of just filling the gap.

Can a non-promo email still sell anything?

Yes, in the P.S. One line, tied to what the email just taught, pointing at the product that fits. The body does the giving. The P.S. is the door, and readers who were made better off by the body walk through it without feeling pushed.
