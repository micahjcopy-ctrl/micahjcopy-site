Email & SMS · Revenue diagnosis

Your email revenue dropped. Your list isn't burnt, your flows are off.

Email revenue is down for the second month and the explanation on the table is that the list is tired. Before you spend a quarter rewriting creative, there are two numbers in your account that usually tell a different story.

A DROP IS MECHANICAL SENT OVER ENTERED FIXED DECAYS, OPERATED COMPOUNDS

Email revenue fell month over month, and the first explanation anyone offers is that the list is burnt. That explanation sends a team off to rewrite creative for a quarter while the real cause keeps running. The cause I find most often isn't the subscribers. A flow got paused, or it's live and reaching a fraction of the people entering it. That's mechanical, and mechanical gets fixed in an afternoon. There's also a third possibility most email people won't raise with you. It's at the end.

A burnt list dies slowly. Your number fell fast.

A list burns the way I describe in the post on batch-and-blast: every campaign goes to everyone, inbox providers learn that most people ignore you, and placement erodes a little more each quarter. It's a slope. It does not show up as a cliff between one month and the next.

When a number falls off a cliff, something in the system changed, and in email accounts the thing that changed is almost never the subscribers. A redesign ships and someone pauses the proven welcome flow. Its replacement is live but under-triggering, so the people who just raised their hand hear nothing. A platform migration archives a flow that nobody rebuilds. An eligibility filter, set two years ago for a reason nobody remembers, quietly excludes most of the people who enter. In every one of those cases the dashboard says the flow is live, because it is. The dashboard doesn't announce what isn't sending.

None of that is the founder's fault. It's what platform defaults and revamp-happy agencies do to an account nobody is watching. I tell the story of one of those paused flows on the leak report page, and I'm not going to repeat the numbers here, because the size of that one leak isn't the point. Where to look first is.

The reframe

Your list isn't burnt. Your flows are off, and fixing them is mechanical, not creative. A sudden drop in email revenue is a settings problem before it's a copy problem, and settings problems come with a checklist.

Why did my email revenue drop? Two numbers answer it.

If the drop was a cliff, you're looking for the thing that changed, and two numbers in your account show it. The first is a ratio. Open your biggest flow and, for the last 30 days, compare how many people entered it with how many were actually sent a message. Divide sent by entered. If that number is under 80 percent, the flow works and the plumbing doesn't. The usual culprits are channel eligibility, quiet hours, suppression rules, or an SMS-only step sitting in front of email subscribers. It's almost always one checkbox. Click into the filters and eligibility settings and read every condition, because the one nobody remembers writing is usually the one doing it.

The second is a comparison you make weekly. Put flow entries next to new signups. Those two lines should move together. If signups climbed and welcome entries didn't, something got paused or broken, and you've caught it in days instead of finding it in a quarterly review. Entries are the number to watch, not sends, because a flow that nobody enters looks identical to a healthy one from the revenue screen.

Both checks take about ten minutes, and between them they split your situation in two. If either comes back ugly, you've found the leak, and the last section is about what happens after you close it. If both come back clean, the drop isn't email's, and that result is the one most people selling email services won't help you with.

When the flows are clean, email isn't the leak

If sent over entered is healthy and entries track signups, the flows are doing their job and the decline is coming from somewhere email can only reflect. I've seen two places.

The first is traffic. In one decline I worked through, the assumption was that retention had collapsed. It hadn't. The repeat rate had held steady from one year to the next. What fell was the count of new customers, and since every returning customer has to be a new customer first, every downstream number followed it down. Fewer people arrive, fewer join the list, fewer enter the flows, and the email line falls with everything else, looking for all the world like a retention problem. If your new-customer count is falling, that's a media buying problem wearing an email costume, and no flow fixes it.

The second is the product, and the buyers your marketing attracts. The moment the transaction happens, marketing loses its power. Everything after that, the second order, the review, the referral, is done by the product. If the first use disappoints, no sequence recovers it. And the audience your ads pull in sets your retention ceiling: a message built around the discount brings in people who leave when the discount does. Email magnifies what's already there. It doesn't manufacture it. For a one-time, big-ticket purchase email is a nice-to-have; for anything people buy again it's essential, and which of those you are is decided by the product, not the platform.

A list that genuinely went cold, because it was blasted for a year, is a different problem again, and the fix for that is a win-back and a sunset, not a creative refresh.

Fixed decays. Operated compounds.

That leaves the owner whose check came back ugly, which in my experience is most of you, and it's the better position to be in. A traffic or product ceiling takes months to move. A checkbox takes an afternoon. You find the paused flow, or the filter, and you turn it back on. Revenue starts recovering within a couple of weeks, because the flow touches every eligible customer automatically. That's the easy part, and it's where most teams stop.

Flows drift. Triggers break in replatforms. A test gets left running with the losing variant still taking half the traffic. Someone pauses a proven flow during the next redesign because its name looked old. The leak you just closed was somebody's unwatched dashboard, and a fix nobody rechecks has a half-life. That's the whole difference between fixing email and operating it. A fixed account decays. An operated one compounds, because the same two numbers from above get read every Monday and the weakest one gets fixed before it costs you a month.

Common questions

Why did my email revenue drop when my list size didn't change?

Because list size isn't what sends email. Flows do. A flow can be live and reaching a fraction of the people entering it, or a proven flow can get paused during a redesign. Check sent divided by entered on your biggest flow, and compare flow entries to new signups, before you blame the list.

How do I know if my email list is actually burnt?

A burnt list shows up as a slow slide in opens and inbox placement over quarters, usually after months of sending every campaign to everyone. A sudden month-over-month drop is almost never that. If engagement has genuinely gone cold, the fix is a win-back and a sunset, not new creative.

Can better email fix a falling repeat-purchase rate?

Only up to the ceiling the product sets. Email magnifies what's already there. If the first use disappoints, or your ads attract discount hunters who leave with the discount, no sequence recovers it. And if new customers are falling, the decline is acquisition showing up in email, not retention.
