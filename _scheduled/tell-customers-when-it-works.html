Tell them when it'll start working.

The refund request says it's been twelve days and I don't notice anything. Your product takes about six weeks. The customer isn't wrong and the product isn't broken. Nobody told them twelve days was early, so they filled the silence with the obvious conclusion.

SILENCE BECOMES DOUBT WEEK ONE TO EIGHT ASK AT HALFWAY

Owners of products that take time to work, a collagen powder, a probiotic, anything the body has to adjust to, tend to carry a belief that costs them customers: the product speaks for itself. It doesn't, not in the first weeks, because in the first weeks there's nothing to hear yet. A customer who doesn't know when to expect the result decides it isn't coming, and they decide early. The fix is a post-purchase email that sets expectations: how and when to use it, what weeks one, two, four and eight look like, and a check-in at the halfway point. It's the cheapest refund prevention you'll ever build.

Why people quit before it works

Say your collagen shows up for most people somewhere around week six. A customer buys it because they want their skin or their joints to feel different. The tub arrives, they take it for ten days, and they look for a change that isn't due for another month. Nothing. It couldn't be anything yet: a product like this works by giving the body something to build with, and the body takes its time, however good the formula is. Now the question forms: is this working? A customer asking that with no answer from you answers it themselves, and almost always against you. The refund isn't a verdict on the product. It's a verdict on the gap between when they expected the result and when it was going to arrive, and you left that gap open.

You can see this in your own numbers. Look at when refunds and subscription cancellations land relative to the order date. Refunds that cluster early, before the product could have done anything, are the signal. They aren't telling you the product fails. They're telling you people stopped before it had a chance, and that's an expectations problem, which means it's yours to fix. And anything you say to a customer about what to expect has to reach them before they start questioning whether it's working, not after, because by then they've already decided.

The rule

A customer will wait for a result they've been told to expect. They won't wait for one they're guessing about. Tell them the timeline before they need it, and you stop getting refunds for a result that wasn't due yet.

The post-purchase email that sets expectations

The email is simple and most stores don't send it. It goes out timed to arrival rather than to the order, because a how-to that beats the box by three days has nothing to act on, and it's forgotten by the time there is. The day the tub lands, so does this: exactly how to take it, when in the day, with what, and what the next eight weeks usually look like.

Then the weeks, each written as something the customer experiences rather than a checkpoint on a chart. Week one, almost nothing you can point to. Keep going. Week two, something you can't quite name, the first hint of a pattern. Week four, the first thing you'd mention to a friend. Week eight, the result you bought it for. The shape I write to is relief, then recognition, then momentum: early on the discomfort eases, in the middle they notice it's a pattern, later they feel like they're moving. Put it in time language, after a few days, by the end of the month, because a sequence the reader can see themselves living is one they'll believe, and a result with no timeline is one they'll doubt. Keep the timeline honest. Promise week two and deliver week six and you've bought yourself a worse refund, with a reason attached.

That one email does three jobs at once. It gets the customer using the product properly, which is the reason anyone reorders at all, as I covered in customers don't reorder what they never used. It makes the second week feel like progress instead of nothing. And it makes you the voice they check against, so when the doubt arrives on day twelve, they remember you said day twelve was early.

The halfway check-in

The timeline email sets the expectation. One more email checks it. Around the midpoint of the supply, send a short note: you ordered about three weeks ago, so you should be roughly halfway through the tub. How are you feeling? That's the whole email. It isn't a pitch and it isn't a survey. It's what a friend who recommended the product would ask.

It does two jobs. For the customer who's drifting, it's a nudge back into the routine at the exact moment they were about to lose it, with a reminder that halfway is still early. For you, the replies are the most useful data in the account. The people who say they're noticing it are ready for the review request and the next offer, which is the signal I make the next offer on instead of a timer. The people who say not yet need another how-to, not a pitch. And the people who say there's too much left over, or that it didn't suit them, are telling you about a fit problem that no timeline fixes, which is the subscription conversation in subscribers are your most valuable customers, and your most fragile. A reply is the cheapest signal you'll get about which group someone's in.

Where your timeline comes from

The weeks above are a pattern, not your product's numbers. Your numbers come from your customers. Ask the ones who stayed: when did you first notice a difference? Put it in a short note to past buyers with a small thank-you attached and you'll get a spread of answers that cluster somewhere. That cluster is your week four, or your week six. Build the timeline email around it. Set the guarantee window past it, so nobody's asked to decide before the result could have shown up. Time the halfway check-in to the middle of it. Get the first month or two under control like this and you've shaped what the customer expects for the whole relationship, which is where longer retention comes from.

Then go back to the refund timing you looked at earlier. If this is working, refunds move later and get fewer, because fewer people are quitting in the dark. That one shift, from the product speaks for itself to tell them when it'll speak, is the difference between a post-purchase flow that sends a receipt and one that keeps the customer. The rest of that flow, the review request and the next product, is in the post-purchase emails that turn one order into ten, and all of it works better once people have stayed long enough to feel the first one work.

Pulling the timeline out of your customers, writing the expectation email, and timing it to delivery is one piece of the retention work on the email & SMS side. Common questions

When should the expectation email go out?

On arrival day, not order day. A how-to that lands while the box is in transit gets forgotten. Send it the day the product lands, then a halfway check-in around the middle of the first supply.

What if I don't know how long my product takes to work?

Ask the customers who stayed when they first noticed a difference. The answers cluster, and that cluster is your timeline. Build the email, the guarantee window and the check-in around it instead of guessing.

Will telling people it takes six weeks hurt sales?

It lowers early refunds, which is where the money goes. An honest timeline reads as confidence, and a customer who knows week two is early keeps going. Promising faster than you deliver buys a worse refund with a reason attached.
