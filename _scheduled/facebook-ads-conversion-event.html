Meta gives you exactly what you ask for.

The leads are cheap and the calendar is full of people who were never going to buy. Or the add-to-carts climb every week and the orders don't. Nothing is broken. You asked Meta for leads, or for carts, and it delivered.

EVENT IS THE INSTRUCTION GATE WHAT FIRES PRICE THE CUSTOMER

The first question I ask in any ad account audit is: what event are you optimizing for, and why? Whether to optimize Facebook ads for leads or purchases sounds like a settings choice. It's the instruction the whole campaign is built on, because Meta uses it twice: to learn from what happens, and to decide who sees the ad in the first place. Ask for cheap leads and the system finds people who fill in forms. Ask for purchases and it starts with people who buy, before it has a single data point from you.

The event is the instruction

Most owners running Meta ads pick an optimization event by what's easy to count. Leads are easy. Add-to-carts are easy. Clicks are easiest of all. And Meta, which is very good at its job, takes the instruction literally. Optimize for clicks and it finds people who click and never buy. Optimize for checkouts and you get checkouts that don't complete. Optimize for leads and the leads come in cheap, because the system is hunting for form-fillers, not buyers.

The rule I run on is simple. The further your optimization event sits from the outcome you actually want, the more targeting and structure you have to do by hand. The closer the event is to revenue, the more you can hand the work to Meta. Even a store with a purchase event isn't safe from this: a store selling $10 and $100 products that optimizes for the lowest cost per purchase gets flooded with $10 orders, because that's exactly what it asked for.

Optimize Facebook ads for purchases when you can

When the data allows it, the answer is purchases. In cases I've studied, one small change, from lead optimization to purchase optimization, has doubled results or scaled profit without a single change to the creative. Same ads, same pages. The creative was never the lever. The instruction was, and it had been pointing at the wrong thing the whole time.

The catch is volume. Purchases are the strongest signal and the scarcest one. A high-ticket offer with a long sales cycle might produce a handful a week, and an ad set optimizing for purchases on that little data gets volatile: strong for a stretch, then a sudden collapse. That's the "as your data allows" part. If you can get purchase volume, ask for purchases. If you can't, you don't fall back to the cheapest event. You build a better one.

The reframe

A more expensive lead that converts is a cheaper customer. Price the customer, not the lead. Optimize as close to revenue as your data allows, and when you can't, take the targeting back by hand.

When you can't: gate the event and hold the budget by hand

Service businesses live here. Purchases usually aren't practical to optimize for, so you optimize for scheduled calls, which Meta tracks reliably. Then it does what it does, and delivers cheap calls rather than qualified ones. The fix is to change what counts. Put a short questionnaire before the booking, screen on the things that matter to you, and fire the "schedule" event only when a lead meets the criteria. Route everyone else to a different page, a different offer, or no call at all. Now Meta is hunting for the people who pass, because those are the only ones it ever sees succeed.

Two details make this work. Write the questions neutrally, with tiered or objective options, because if it's obvious which answer gets you in, people lie to get at the pricing and your data goes bad. And put the budget at the ad set level rather than the campaign level. A campaign-level budget will chase the cheapest lead across your ad sets every time; ad set budgets let you move spend by hand toward the ad sets that produce actual customers, which you can only know from your own sales data, not the dashboard. For a webinar, the same logic points at a mid-funnel event, attendees or people who watched a set number of minutes, which carries more meaning than a registration and more volume than a sale.

The quality step costs you leads on paper. That's the point. A lead that costs more and closes is a cheaper customer, and the only number that should decide this is cost per customer, with the follow-up in place that turns a qualified lead into one.

None of it works if the event lies

Every instruction above assumes the event fires when a real person does the real thing. Set up proper event tracking before you touch the optimization setting, because an event that fires on a page view, or fires twice, or fires on your own tests, teaches Meta to find the wrong people with great confidence. Use the Test Events tool in Events Manager when you're checking a setup; a test conversion that lands in the live event may not show in Ads Manager, but Meta still sees it and learns from it in the background.

The same rule covers value optimization. If you want Meta to chase higher-value customers, it has to see the whole order: the bump, the upsell, the second product. Feed it the front end alone and it will underweight your revenue and drift back toward the cheapest conversion, because that's what you showed it. Meta optimizes toward what it can see. And a gated event will fire less often than an open one, which is fine: for the event you optimize toward, a small, recent, consistent stream beats raw volume, the reason Learning Limited is a check-engine light rather than a failure.

The check takes ten minutes. Open Events Manager and, for each campaign, write down the event it optimizes for. Then ask one question about each: when a real person does this, are they a customer yet? If the answer is no, move the event one step closer to the sale, by switching to purchases where the volume exists, or by gating the event where it doesn't. And if your ads already get clicks and no sales, check the hand-off to the page first, because no event fixes a page that breaks the promise.

Common questions

Should I optimize Facebook ads for leads or purchases?

Purchases, whenever you have the volume to keep the ad set stable. When you don't, optimize for the closest trackable step to revenue and gate it, so the event only fires for a lead who meets your criteria.

Why are my Facebook leads cheap but bad?

Because you asked for leads. Meta found people who fill in forms, and it will keep finding more of them. Gate the event behind a short qualifying questionnaire, judge ad sets on cost per customer, and move budget by hand toward the ones that close.

Should I use a campaign budget or ad set budgets for lead generation?

Ad set budgets when you're not optimizing for purchases. A campaign-level budget chases the cheapest lead across your ad sets; ad set budgets let you shift spend toward the ad sets that produce real customers.
