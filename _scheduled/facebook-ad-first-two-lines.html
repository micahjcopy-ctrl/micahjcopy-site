The first two lines are the whole ad.

Your ad stalled, so you rewrote it. New angle, new body, new close. The numbers barely moved, because on a phone the reader sees two lines and a grey "see more" before deciding whether any of it is for them. That's the part you didn't change, and it's the only part that decides who reads the rest.

THE FOLD DECIDES CALLOUT, PAIN, OUTCOME TEST INTROS, NOT ADS

The primary text of a Facebook ad shows a couple of lines on a phone and then a "see more" link. If those lines don't grab the right person, nothing after them gets read. So the body you spent an afternoon on is read only by people the first two lines already won. When an ad stalls, the instinct is to rebuild the ad. The cheaper move, and the one that actually moves cost per acquisition, is to leave the body alone and test the intro.

Where the ad actually dies

Picture your ad in the feed. The image, then two lines of text, then "see more". The reader decides in that half-second whether this is for them. In an ad, unlike a sales page, nobody arrived with intent. Nothing is owed to you yet. The intro has to earn attention from the right person before it can do anything else, and if it doesn't, the thumb keeps moving and the rest of your copy never loads in anyone's head.

That's why rewriting the body rarely fixes a stalled ad. You changed what the already convinced were reading. In one account I studied, two core ads were kept winning for months at high spend by cycling through more than sixty intros and images while the body stayed put. In another, swapping the intros and images on an existing ad cut cost per acquisition in half. Same offer, same body copy. Different first two lines.

What goes in the two lines

I write intros as a recipe with four ingredients. A callout, which names who the ad is for: women over fifty, moms of addicts, owners of a two-location dental practice. A pain, the thing they feel this week. An outcome, what changes. And the deeper why behind the outcome, because nobody wants to lose twelve pounds; they want the clothes in the closet to fit and the energy at four in the afternoon. Two connectors handle the objection inside the same sentence: "without" and "even if". Lose the weight without counting calories. Grow the list even if you're starting from zero.

Lead with your strongest ingredient and keep it in every variation. If one claim has out-pulled everything else you've run, it goes in every intro and you vary what sits around it. Then put each line through the check I use before I spend a dollar on it. Pain with no outcome reads as fear, and people scroll away from fear. Outcome with no pain reads as fluff, and people don't believe fluff. The line has to carry both: something has changed, and because of that change, something is possible for this reader now.

The first line of your primary text is for a stranger

There's a catch in the recipe, and it's the one that sinks most of the intros I review. Cold traffic isn't invested in you. A brand people already recognize can open with curiosity and get the tap. An unknown brand can't. It has to say who this is for, what it is and why it's different, fast, in words that need no decoding.

Two openings for the same program, one for mothers of addicted children, make the point. "It was the worst day of my life" is emotional and goes nowhere, because it could be anyone's worst day. "Mom of an addict?" is four words and it has already found its reader. The same rule catches the vocabulary you're too close to see. A chocolate maker in one teardown I studied led every ad with "hand-scooped", a phrase translated from German that means nothing to an English buyer, when "made from scratch, not remelted blocks" was sitting right there. And in health, don't open with the diagnosis. Open by stacking the symptoms the reader recognizes, the mid-afternoon crash, the food that suddenly disagrees with them, the sleep that doesn't restore, and only then introduce the cause. People engage when they see their own week reflected back. Credentials go nowhere near the first line. The letters after your name and the years in business wait until the reader has a reason to care.

The callout is the targeting

Naming who the ad is for does a second job, and it's the reason I'm strict about the callout: it does more than win a click. Meta reads your copy before it serves an impression, and once the ad is running, the people who respond teach it who to find next. The platform optimizes for whoever completes the event you gave it, not for quality. Write an intro that appeals to a low-intent reader and you'll attract that reader and train the system to bring you more of them. So the filter has to live in the words, and a qualifier that costs you clicks is often doing its job. An ad I studied for a side-business program said plainly that it needed a thousand dollars up front and basic computer skills; the people that line turned away were never going to buy, and the people it kept arrived already knowing the terms. That's the whole argument of why your Meta ads don't have a targeting problem, and the intro is where it bites hardest. The first line is the audience setting you actually control.

Test intros, not ads

So the method. Write the full copy first, because the body sets the tone and the promise. Then write the intros as the final layer, three or four of them, each built from the recipe with one ingredient swapped. "Without counting calories" against "without giving up dessert". The pain led, then the outcome led. The same phrases reordered, which shifts results more than you'd expect. Pair them with two or three images and run all of it in one ad set, so Meta can push spend toward the winners, or give each about 1,500 impressions and read cost per click, since the intro's only job is a click from the right person.

Two practical notes. Turn off Meta's copy "enhancements", which rearrange your headline and primary text and can pull an old page description in from your site, so you end up testing a line you didn't write. And keep the intro test separate from the page problem. If a new intro wins the click and sales still don't come, you've moved from an ad problem to a page problem, which is a different diagnosis: clicks but no sales. Intros are one axis of the larger creative testing system. They're the axis that costs the least and tells you the most, which is why they come first.

Common questions

How long should the first line of a Facebook ad be?

Short enough to show in full above "see more" on a phone, roughly two lines of primary text. It needs a callout to the person it's for and the pain or the outcome, in words that need no decoding. If a stranger can't tell in that space whether the ad is for them, the rest never gets read.

Should I use a curiosity hook in my ad's intro?

Only if people already know your brand. Cold traffic isn't invested in you yet, so vague intrigue fails for unknown brands. Say who it's for, what it is and why it's different first. A sentence cut at the "see more" break can open a loop, but sparingly, and only after the callout has done its job.

How many intros should I test at once?

Two or three intros against two or three images, six to nine ads in one ad set, is a manageable first test. Swap one ingredient per variation so you learn what moved. Give each around 1,500 impressions and judge on cost per click, or let Meta allocate the spend and read the winners in aggregate.
