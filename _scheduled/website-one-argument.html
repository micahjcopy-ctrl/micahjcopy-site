A good website reads like one argument, not a stack of sections.

Three proposals on your desk, and they all list the same sections: hero, about, services, testimonials, FAQ, contact. The prices differ. The structure doesn't. That's the first sign none of them is planning to build you a sales argument, because an argument can't be listed in advance. It has to be made.

SECTIONS ARE NOT A STRUCTURE EACH ONE EARNS THE NEXT RUN THE PULL-OUT TEST

A website structure that converts is a chain of thought, not a set of sections: every section picks up what the one before it left open and sets up the one after. I run one test on every page I write and every proposal I'm asked to judge. Pull a section out. If the page reads about the same without it, that section was stacked on, not built in, and the page is weaker than it looks.

Why the standard section list fails

You can tell weak copy from great copy by how easily you can pull sections out of it. In weak copy you can remove a block, rearrange the rest, swap in something generic, and it reads about the same, because the sections were never connected. They were stacked on top of each other. In great copy you can't, because each thought depends on the one before it. If a section can be removed without weakening the page, you have to ask why it was there in the first place. That's my standing rule for every piece of copy I write, and it's how I can judge a copywriter's skill from a glance at their work.

The standard section list produces stacked pages by design. A template hands the builder six boxes and the builder fills them, so the About section says what any About section says, the testimonials row could sit on any site, and the FAQ answers questions nobody on this page was asking. None of it is wrong. None of it is connected either, and a visitor's attention is a thread. Every section either continues the thread or drops it, and a dropped thread is a closed tab. So the question for any page is whether each section continues the thought, not whether it has the right sections. And there's one spot where the thread breaks more than anywhere else.

The connector: where most pages break

The headline makes a promise. The first section below the fold has one job, which is to continue the story the headline started. I call it the connector, and it frames how everything further down gets read. It should show the result isn't a fluke, name the problem, say why the usual solution falls short, and set up how yours does better. That's a lot of weight for one section, which is why most pages put a row of service icons there instead and lose the reader on the spot.

In one case I studied, a headline was tightened to name a specific audience and a subhead was added with a direct, timed benefit. Below-the-fold engagement roughly doubled. Nothing below the fold had changed; the connector finally had a promise worth continuing. The reverse happens too. Put a stronger headline on a stacked page and the break doesn't disappear, it moves lower, because the sections below are still built for the old promise and the order of persuasion stops making sense partway down. Your homepage headline is the first link in the chain and the connector is the second. What follows them is the shape of the argument itself.

The rule

Every section picks up the thought the last one left open and leaves one open for the next. If you can remove a section and the page reads the same, it shouldn't be there. A transition sentence pasted on top doesn't count; it hides the seam without fixing it.

The website structure that converts: three agreements

An argument on a page wins three agreements, in order. First the visitor agrees they have the problem as you've described it. Then they agree your kind of solution is the right kind. Only then do they agree your specific product is the one to buy. Each agreement has to be won before the next can start, and each one loses people. The way I picture it: a hundred arrive, something like eighty are still reading after they agree on the problem, and roughly ten reach the product and buy. Skip an agreement and the numbers below it collapse, because you're asking someone to accept a product for a problem they haven't admitted to yet. The connector is where the first agreement starts being won, which is why a page that opens below the fold with a row of service icons has skipped straight to the third, and why so few people reach the product.

Once you see the page as three agreements, the standard sections stop being sections and become evidence placed where it's needed. Testimonials aren't a row. A single review belongs beside the agreement it helps win, usually the last one, which I cover in reviews people actually believe. The FAQ answers the objection the previous section raised, not a generic list. The About section earns a place at the moment the argument needs "why should I believe you," and not before. Everything on the page is either winning one of the three agreements or it's in the way. Which gives you a way to check your own page without being a copywriter.

Run the pull-out test on your own site

Open your homepage and do four things to each section. Pull it out: read the page without it, and if nothing after it loses its setup and no promise goes unpaid, it was stacked. Then ask which of the three agreements it was helping to win; if you can't name one, it was decoration. Move it: if it reads just as well two sections lower, it isn't tied to its neighbors. Swap it: if a generic version, any FAQ, any testimonial row, any "who it's for," would do the same job, it isn't part of this chain yet. Then check the hand-offs: does each section pick up what the last one left open and leave something open for the next? A transition sentence pasted on top doesn't count. It hides the seam without fixing it.

Score the chain, not the sections. A page of strong sections that can be shuffled is still a weak page. The cuts are the easy half; that's the same discipline as cutting before you add. The harder half is rewriting what's left so each section depends on the one before it.

And it gives you the question for those three proposals. Ask each builder which section of their plan they'd cut, and what the section after it would lose. Someone filling a template can't answer, because in a template nothing depends on anything. Someone building an argument will tell you what each section sets up and what breaks without it. That answer is worth more than the mockups.

Common questions

What sections should a website have?

Whatever the argument needs, in order: a promise, a connector that continues it, the problem, why the usual fix falls short, your solution, the product, and proof beside the agreement it helps win. A template list produces sections that can be shuffled, which means they aren't connected.

What is the pull-out test?

Remove each section in turn. If nothing after it loses its setup and no promise goes unpaid, the section was stacked, not connected: cut it or rewrite it until something depends on it. Then try moving it, then swapping in a generic version. Score the chain, not the sections.

How do I tell a web designer who fills templates from one who builds an argument?

Ask which section of their proposal they would cut and what the section after it would lose. A template-filler can't answer, because nothing in a template depends on anything else. A builder will tell you what each section sets up and what breaks without it.
