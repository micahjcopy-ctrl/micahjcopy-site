Do you actually need retargeting?

The proposal arrived with a retargeting campaign as its own line item and the word "essential" next to it. You sell a forty-dollar product. Before you sign, it's worth knowing that Meta already shows your ads to warm people inside the campaign you're running, and that a separate budget for it earns its keep in some businesses and quietly pays twice in others.

BROAD ALREADY RETARGETS TICKET SIZE DECIDES NUDGE, NOT RESCUE

Retargeting was once the easiest win in Facebook ads: tag everyone who visited, chase them with a discount, count the sales. Three things changed and the playbook didn't. Now the question isn't whether to retarget, because the broad campaign is already doing it. The question is whether a dedicated budget is worth it: whether it adds sales that wouldn't have happened anyway. The answer depends on what you sell.

What changed since retargeting was a must-have

Three shifts. The iOS privacy changes broke the tracking that made retargeting audiences precise, so the list of "people who visited" is fuzzy now and the exclusions are fuzzier. The cost of an impression went up, so every repeat view has to justify itself. And Meta's delivery changed: inside an ordinary broad campaign it now re-serves your ad to warm people on its own, with no separate audience built. In tests I've studied,letting the platform handle cold and warm together in one broad ad set with minimal exclusions often beat a hand-built retargeting structure.

That last shift is the one that matters for the proposal on your desk. If the broad campaign already reaches your cart abandoners, a second campaign aimed at the same people is often paying twice for the same sale. So the honest test for a retargeting line item is whether it moves people who wouldn't otherwise have moved, in a way you can see. Whether it can pass that test depends first on your ticket.

Is retargeting worth it? Your ticket size answers first

For low-ticket products, the kind bought on impulse, it usually isn't. A no on a $27 product is a no; seeing it again a day later rarely changes the outcome, and the margin can't carry repeated exposure at today's impression costs. You also can't separate the sales the retargeting caused from the ones that were coming anyway, so the campaign reports a return it didn't earn. The classic discount ad to cart abandoners has lost most of its force, and it carries a cost of its own: the customer who paid full price yesterday sees the discount today. For a low-ticket store, the abandoned cart email flow does the reminding for the cost of a send, with the objection-handling built in, which is why I build that before I'd propose ads for the same job.

High-ticket is the opposite case. When the purchase is considered, the buyer needs objections cleared before they'll move, and a retargeting ad that clears one is doing visible work. Webinar funnels and multi-step offers, where someone opted in and now needs a nudge to book the call, are the same shape. And launches, live events and big sales to a warm list are the best use of all: time-sensitive and high-value, aimed at people who are already pre-qualified. During a cart-open week the play is to push hard at the warm pool across several objectives at once, because one or two high-ticket sales pays for hundreds of dollars of retargeting.

Two exceptions worth knowing

The first is catalog size. If you sell hundreds of products, dynamic product ads, which show each visitor the exact item they looked at, earn their budget even at modest prices, because the reminder is specific and the audience is large. A single-product store doesn't need them. A Shopify store with four hundred SKUs probably does, and that's the one low-ticket case where I'd put ads next to the email flow rather than instead of it.

The second is the awkward one. When I start work on an account, a small bottom-of-funnel retargeting campaign is sometimes the fastest way to show a result in the first weeks, because the warmest people are the easiest to move. I'll say so when I propose it. What it isn't is a strategy, and on a low-ticket offer the email flow still does that job for less. A quick win is a reason to run it for a month. It's not a reason to keep paying for it.

The test

A dedicated retargeting budget earns its keep when the purchase is considered enough that a nudge changes the outcome, and when you can see it change. Impulse buys fail the first half. Fuzzy tracking fails the second.

If you run it, run it as a nudge, not a rescue

When the case is there, the creative has one job: clear the path to the next step. Not discounts. Objections. I pair each objection with the testimonial that answers it. "I'm too old for this" gets the customer in her eighties. "This looks complicated" gets the beginner who had it working in a weekend. Two frames carry most of it: "without", as in results without giving up dessert, and "even if", as in results even if you're starting from nothing. The one frame I avoid is money, because "even if you're broke" fills the funnel with people who will never move up to the offer.

Structure stays small. Combine every warm audience, visitors, engagers, the email list, into one ad set at a modest daily budget and optimize it for purchases, because a retargeting set optimized for clicks just finds the people who click. Watch frequency: two to four views a person over seven days is fine, and at seven to ten the returns drop while the annoyance climbs. And for a considered offer, a belief layer at five or ten dollars a day, testimonials and simple carousels aimed at the people who visited and didn't opt in, or booked and didn't buy, keeps them warm until they're ready. In one coaching business I studied, close to half of sales came from people who first said no. That's the pool a nudge is for, and it's the only pool where the nudge is worth paying for.

Judge it like any other ad set

A retargeting ad set doesn't get a pass because the audience is warm. It gets judged the way every ad set does, spend against target CPA across three windows, with the same kill and scale rules I use everywhere else in a Meta ads account. The one extra question is the incremental one, and it's hard to answer cleanly. The proper version holds out a region and watches what happens to sales there, which few businesses can run. The crude version on a small account is to pause the retargeting set for a stretch and watch blended sales. If they hold, the broad campaign was already doing the job and you've been paying twice. If they dip, the line item is earning its place.

So before you sign the proposal, answer two questions. What does a customer pay you, and how many products do you sell. A considered purchase or a big catalog: run it as a nudge and judge it hard. An impulse product with a handful of SKUs: send the budget to the broad campaign and let the email flow do the chasing.

Common questions

Does Meta retarget automatically inside a broad campaign?

Yes. Inside an ordinary broad campaign Meta re-serves your ads to warm people, visitors and engagers, without a separate audience. In tests I've studied,one broad ad set with minimal exclusions often beat a hand-built retargeting structure, so a separate budget has to prove it adds sales.

Should I run discount ads to cart abandoners?

Usually not. The discount ad to abandoners has lost most of its force, and it shows a price cut to people who paid full price yesterday. Low-ticket stores should let the cart email flow do the reminding. For considered purchases, retarget with the objection and the testimonial that answers it.

What frequency is too high for retargeting ads?

Two to four views per person over seven days is fine for a warm audience. At seven to ten and above, returns drop and annoyance climbs. Keep warm audiences together in one ad set at a modest budget so frequency stays sane.
