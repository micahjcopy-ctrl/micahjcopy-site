Paid Media · Social proof

Your ad's comments are part of your ad.

Under your ad right now there's a customer saying it worked, someone asking about shipping, and a stranger calling it a scam. You've been deleting the bad ones and ignoring the rest. Last week you duplicated the ad into a new campaign and it started again from zero, with none of them. Every one of those moves cost you sales.

BUYERS READ COMMENTS META READS THEM TOO PROTECT THE POST ID

Comments on a Meta ad are the only part of it the market writes. They do two jobs you never assigned them: they sell to the next person who reads them, and they tell Meta who that next person should be. Treat them as noise and you're leaving the most believable copy on the ad unmanaged. Duplicate the ad the usual way and you throw that copy out.

People read the comments before they read your offer

Imagine a television ad that let every viewer scribble a verdict under it for the next viewer to see. That's a Facebook ad. People scan the comments for proof before they buy, usually without noticing they're doing it, and a thread with dozens of "is this a scam" replies or dozens of "mine arrived in two days" replies decides the sale before your headline does. It matters most where trust is the whole purchase: personal brands, coaching, anything sold to an older or skeptical audience. In one example I keep in my notes, it was the thoughtful replies under an ad for a pet stain remover that reassured older buyers it actually worked.

The comments are doing the job testimonials do on your website, except they're doing it at the moment of the scroll, in the voice of someone who isn't you. That's why they convert, and why nobody skips them.

Comments tell Meta who to show the ad to

The second job is the one almost nobody manages. Meta counts every comment, positive or negative, as engagement, and it reads who is engaging. Then it finds more of them. In one business I looked at, a wave of Christian-themed comments appeared under an ad, and within days there was a surge of Christian users booking calls, though faith had nothing to do with the offer. A health ad picked up an off-topic comment about feet, the thread spiraled, and the ad started pulling the wrong crowd until performance dropped. The thread steers the audience. So moderation is about keeping your traffic qualified, not about looking tidy.

There's a ranking trap on top. Meta sorts comments by "most relevant" by default, shaped by engagement and friend connections, so not everyone sees the same thread, and one early negative comment with a few replies under it can sit at the top for everyone. Which means the thread isn't something you watch. It's something you steer.

The two jobs

Comments are an input, not a by-product. They sell to the next reader, and they tell Meta who the next reader should be. Both of those are your job, and both are lost the moment you duplicate the ad.

How to manage comments on Facebook ads without wrecking the signal

Four moves, in the order I use them.

  • Nurture the good threads. Reply under positive comments, ask the commenter a question, tag people in. That engagement pushes those threads to "most relevant", and it's the flywheel that buries the negative ones without touching them.
  • Hide, don't delete. A hidden comment still shows to the person who wrote it and their friends, so the engagement count stays and nobody is provoked into a second round. Delete only what's obscene.
  • Block persistent trolls. This is the single most effective move. A troll who keeps commenting keeps getting served your ads, because Meta reads their activity as interest, and spreads across your campaigns. Blocking stops them seeing the page and hides every past comment they've left, at once. A persistent troll never converts, so nothing is lost.
  • Automate the floor. Meta's moderation rules can auto-hide comments from accounts with no profile picture or a very new history, anything carrying a link or an image, and keywords like "scam" including the misspelled versions. In cases I've studied, brands saw their cost per acquisition drop meaningfully from controlling comment quality alone.

Genuine complaints are a different thing from trolling: answer those in a direct message, or clarify the facts publicly, depending on the tone. And don't kill an ad because the comments turned angry. Rage fuels engagement and reach, so an argumentative thread often widens delivery while it runs. What hurts is hides and reports, which raise your costs long before any flag, and readers who are put off, which shows up as falling click-through and conversion. So judge the ad on the same spend-against-target read I use for every kill decision. Compliance intact and numbers holding, it runs.

Duplicating your ad throws the proof away

Now the part that undoes everything above. When you duplicate an ad the normal way, or when you edit a proven ad's copy, image or headline, Meta creates a new post. The comments, the likes and the shares stay on the old one. Your new campaign starts from zero, and because the two are now separate ads to Meta, the engagement splits and weakens on both. In another account, an ad heavy with social proof beat every better-written variation the team made, on nothing but the thread it carried. That's the asset you lose by hitting duplicate.

The fix is the post ID. Every ad is a post, and "Use existing post" lets you run the same post across ad sets, campaigns and objectives with its whole thread attached. Preview the ad, open "See Facebook post with comments", copy the number at the end of the URL, and put that number in the ad's name so you can find it later. Never edit a proofed ad; turn it off and save it, or reuse its post ID somewhere new. This also settles a question that comes up when you remix a winner: the new formats start with no proof, so the original keeps running on its post ID alongside them until one of them earns a thread of its own. And if a thread ever gets hijacked past saving, a fresh post ID is the reset.

Build the proof on purpose

Once the proof is protected, make more of it. My practice is to take the conversion ad's post ID and run a cheap engagement campaign on it aimed at past buyers. They comment, they answer each other's questions, and they defend the product when a stranger calls it a scam, because they own it and it works. The thread fills with the only testimonials that read as real, the unprompted ones. Then the same post ID carries that thread into every campaign the ad runs in, cold or warm. Ask happy customers to comment, too; most never think to.

One last thing for anyone hiring this out. Comment management rarely has its own line on a quote, but it can be scoped into one if you raise it up front, so ask whether it's in scope before you sign. A thread left unmanaged before a launch is the kind of thing that snowballs, and a nervous owner reading three negatives as proof something's wrong is exactly the moment the work pays for itself.

Common questions

Should I delete negative comments on my Facebook ads?

Hide them instead. A hidden comment still shows to its author and their friends, so the engagement count stays and nobody is provoked, while the public thread stays clean. Delete only obscene content, and block anyone who keeps coming back, which hides all their past comments at once.

Does duplicating a Facebook ad keep its comments and likes?

Not the normal way. Duplicating or editing a proven ad creates a new post with zero engagement, and the two become separate ads that split the proof. Use "Use existing post" with the original's post ID to run it in new ad sets, campaigns or objectives with its whole thread attached.

Should I turn off an ad that is getting angry comments?

Usually not. Angry threads fuel engagement and reach while they run. The real risk is click-through and conversion falling because readers are put off, so judge the ad on spend against your target, keep it compliant, and block the persistent trolls rather than killing the ad.
