Paid Media · Click diagnosis

Your Facebook ads get clicks. Nobody buys. It's the page, not the ad.

The click-through rate is healthy, the clicks are cheap, and the purchases column is close to empty. So you rewrite the ad, because the ad is the thing you can see. The ad already did its job. The problem starts the moment someone lands.

CTR IS A CLUE CHECK THE PLUMBING PAGE BREAKS THE PROMISE

Two ads I studied side by side settled this for me. One pulled an 8% click-through rate and lost money, because the people it attracted never bought. The other was a 2,000-word text post you had to tap "see more" to read; it pulled 0.35% and won, because the few who clicked were exactly the right people. Facebook ads with a high CTR and no conversions aren't a mystery. CTR was never the result. It's a clue, and when it's high while sales are flat it points somewhere specific: at the gap between what the ad promised and what the page delivered.

The number you can't pay the mortgage with

This is how I read a Meta ads account. Cost per purchase against the ceiling you set before launch is the verdict, and I've written about where that ceiling comes from. Return on ad spend, with enough purchases behind it to trust, is the verdict. Everything else, CTR, CPM, CPC, frequency, is a diagnostic: useful for working out why the verdict came in the way it did, and useless as a decision on its own. I can't pay the mortgage with a click-through rate, and neither can you.

The surface numbers are seductive because they arrive first and they're easy to improve. A good CTR with cheap clicks tells you the ad stopped the scroll and the traffic was inexpensive. That's all it tells you. A low CPM often means Meta is buying you cheap, low-value attention; a high CPM often means it's reaching people more likely to buy, who cost more because every advertiser wants them. Good surface numbers can sit on top of a funnel that's leaking all the way down, and the ad looks great right up until you check the bank. So the first question when CTR is high and sales are flat isn't what's wrong with the page. It's whether the clicks are reaching the page at all.

Before you blame the page, check the plumbing

When a funnel that was converting stops, or never starts, I check the tech before I check the copy. One account I studied had 5,600 link clicks in an ad set and about 100 landing page views. Not a page problem. The ads were sending traffic to the wrong URL, a step built for organic visitors rather than the page built for the ad, and the pixel wasn't seeing most of what arrived. Every "the page doesn't convert" theory was wrong, because almost nobody was reaching the page.

So compare two columns before anything else: link clicks against landing page views. A wide gap means the clicks aren't landing, and the usual causes are a wrong or outdated URL on one ad, a page that loads too slowly on a phone, or a pixel that isn't firing where it should. Open each ad and check the link by hand. Then confirm the events in Events Manager. If the clicks aren't arriving, you don't have a conversion problem yet. You have a plumbing problem, and it's a ten-minute fix that looks like a strategy crisis.

Why Facebook ads get a high CTR and no conversions

Once the clicks are landing and nobody buys, the diagnosis gets narrow. Strong CTR with weak conversion is almost always an ad-to-page disconnect, and it happens in one of two ways.

The first is that the ad called out the wrong person. The 8% ad I opened with did this: a hook that anyone would tap, which brought in readers rather than buyers. Your words tell Meta who to find, which is why the creative is the targeting, so a click-rich, buyer-poor ad is often selecting for the wrong crowd by design. The tell in the numbers is a decent CTR, say 3 to 5%, sitting next to a surprisingly high cost per click, in one case I looked at around $9. CPC tells you more than CTR here: when Meta charges that much for a click, it's reaching people who are expensive and still not buying, which means the ad and the page are pulling in different directions.

The second is that the ad made a promise the page doesn't keep. The ad says "land a higher-paying job in a few hours a week"; the page says "switch careers" and talks about modules. The ad leads with a plain outcome; the page opens on a term the reader has to decode. The ad was written for someone who already knows the problem; the page was written for someone who doesn't. Each of those is a broken hand-off, and the reader feels it in the first two seconds and leaves. What converts a clicked visitor is a page that restates the exact promise, in the same words, and then answers the silent question every ad raises, "is this real?", with proof that shows a transformation rather than praise.

The reframe

Cost per purchase against your target is the verdict. CTR, CPM and CPC are clues. A high CTR with no sales is the clue that says the ad opened a door the page didn't walk through.

Split it: traffic, funnel, sales

The reason owners rewrite the ad is that the whole thing looks like one problem. It's three. Traffic is whether the right people arrive at the right cost. Funnel is whether the page turns them into a lead or a sale. Sales is whether the lead becomes money. Read them as separate stages and the high-CTR, no-purchase account diagnoses itself: traffic is fine, funnel is broken, so the fix lives on the page. The same reading works the other way for a service business: bookings and show-ups strong but closes weak means the ads and the page did their jobs, and the problem is on the call. When traffic is on target and conversions are down, you fix the page. You don't spend more, because more traffic into a page that breaks the promise only breaks it for more people, which is the point of you don't have a traffic problem.

Do this today. Put the ad and the landing page up side by side. Read the first two lines of the ad, then the headline of the page. If the page doesn't pick up the ad's promise in the same words, that's the fix, and it costs a headline, not a new campaign. Then read on and ask what the page shows that would make a stranger believe the promise. If the answer is a list of features, you've found the second fix.

Common questions

Is a high CTR on Facebook ads a good sign?

Only as a clue. It tells you the ad stops the scroll. It doesn't tell you whether the people clicking will buy; an 8% CTR ad can lose and a 0.35% one can win. Judge the ad on cost per purchase against your target.

Why don't my link clicks match my landing page views?

Usually a wrong URL on one ad, a page loading too slowly on mobile, or a pixel that isn't firing. Check the link on every ad by hand and confirm the events in Events Manager before you change anything else.

Should I change the audience when clicks don't convert?

No. The ad already picked the audience; the words called out whoever is clicking. If the clicks are landing and nobody buys, fix the hand-off between the ad's promise and the page, and if the ad is attracting readers rather than buyers, rewrite the callout.
