First-time, repeat and VIP buyers need different emails.

Your campaign went to "Customers." In that bucket was someone who bought a bag of your dog food last Tuesday and someone on her ninth. They got the same subject line, the same offer and the same 10 percent off, and only one of them had a reason to open it.

ORDER COUNT DECIDES ONE JOB PER TIER BEING VERSUS BECOMING

Two people are in your "Customers" list. One bought a single bag of your dog food last Tuesday and hasn't opened it. The other is on her ninth bag, has written you a review and tells her neighbors. Last week they received the same email. A segment only earns its name when the people in it share a behavior, because the behavior is what tells you what they need to hear next. After the purchase, the behavior that separates those two isn't engagement. It's how many times they've bought, and that number decides the email.

"Customers" isn't a segment. It's a filing cabinet.

The first segment every list needs is the one in the post on batch-and-blast: engaged versus not, so promotions go to people who are paying attention and deliverability stops bleeding. That segment sorts people by whether they're listening. It says nothing about what to say to them, and after the purchase, that's the question that matters.

Look at what the two dog-food buyers actually share. Nothing. The first-timer cost you an acquisition and hasn't earned it back; the only thing that matters with her is the second bag, and the subscription if you can get it. The ninth-bag buyer paid for herself a long time ago. What she wants is to be treated like someone on her ninth bag, and "10% off your next order" tells her you have no idea who she is. The store that wins over the next few years is the one with a base that buys multiple times, not the one with the most first orders, and a single "customers" list treats that base like strangers.

The split

After the purchase, order count is the behavior that matters. A first-timer, a repeat buyer, a VIP and a lapsed buyer each need a different next email, because each of them is in a different place with you.

Segment customers by purchase history: four buckets, four jobs

Start with the two buyers from the top and fill in the tiers between them. First-time buyers, one order. Acquisition is already paid, so every dollar they spend from here is the cheap kind. Their emails do one thing: get the product used, then get the second order. How-to, the story behind the brand, a review request once they've felt it, and the subscription push, which for a consumable is the single biggest opportunity in this bucket. The consumption piece is what why customers don't reorder covers, and it lives here.

Repeat buyers, two to five orders. They've decided you're real. The job shifts from education to expansion: the complement to what they already buy, the bundle, the review-and-photo request with a small incentive that turns a happy customer into proof and a sale in the same email.

VIPs, five orders and up. This is a different relationship. Early access before anything goes public, the perk nobody else gets, the ask for their opinion on what's next. The job is to extend the window they're actively buying in, and recognition does that better than any discount.

Lapsed buyers, bought before, quiet for 90 to 180 days. Not the same as a cold subscriber. They know the product, they chose to stop, and a discount tells them the thing they stopped buying is now worth less. Lead with novelty: what's new, what changed, what they haven't seen. The reason to come back is that something is different.

Four buckets, four jobs. The emails you'd write for each barely overlap, which is the point, and it's also why a one-list store's "customer" email ends up saying nothing to anyone.

"You are" keeps them. "You are becoming" moves them up.

The buckets tell you what to say. The job tells you how to say it, and there are two voices. Affirmation is "you are": you arrived, you're the kind of person who does this, here's what you came for. That voice keeps people. Aspiration is "you are becoming": you're on your way somewhere, here's the next version of you. That voice moves people up a tier.

So the VIP email is affirmation. You're one of the people this brand is actually for, and here's what that gets you. Telling a VIP she's becoming something is an insult; she's already there. The first-timer email is aspiration. You're three weeks from being someone whose dog doesn't have the problem that brought you here, and bag two is how you get there. Telling a first-timer "you are" bounces off, because she doesn't believe it yet. Aspiration doesn't ask her to. It only asks her to believe in where she's going.

Repeat buyers get both, in that order: affirm what they've built, then open the door to the next tier. Underneath all of it is identity, and identity is the real retention engine. Once someone sees herself as the kind of person who feeds her dog this, the reorder stops being a decision, and you can keep serving that identity for as long as she holds it.

Build it from the order count you already have

None of this needs a data team. Order count is already a field in your store and your email platform. Four segments on that field, plus a 14-day exclusion for anyone who just bought, so a new customer isn't hit with a campaign on day two. Then one email per bucket this week, written in the voice its job calls for. The VIP one is the easiest, because it's pure affirmation and you already know who they are. The first-timer one is the hardest, because aspiration has to be specific to be believed. Those four become the split every future campaign runs against, and the day you send a launch, the VIPs see it first, the first-timers see it framed as a next step, and the lapsed see it as the thing that changed.

The discount trap is worth naming, because it's where one-list stores default. The same 10 percent to everyone trains the whole base to wait, the slow damage in why constant sales kill a store. Spread across the tiers it looks worse. The first-timer who used a welcome code gets a second one and learns you always have one. The VIP gets the same code as a stranger. The lapsed buyer gets told the product is cheaper now. Early access costs nothing in margin and does the VIP job better. Novelty does the lapsed job better. The second order comes from consumption, not from a code. Once the four buckets exist, most of the reasons to discount disappear with them.

Setting up the buyer tiers and writing the first email for each is a two-week job I run at the start of most retention accounts, because every campaign afterward inherits the split. If everyone on your list still gets the same email after they buy, that's what the email & SMS service fixes first.

Common questions

How should I segment customers by purchase history?

By order count: first-time buyers (one order), repeat buyers (two to five), VIPs (five or more), and lapsed buyers (bought before, quiet for 90 to 180 days). Each gets a different next email: consumption and the second order, expansion, early access and recognition, and what's new.

Is this different from segmenting by engagement?

Yes. The engaged segment sorts people by whether they're opening and clicking, and it protects deliverability. Purchase-history segments sort buyers by how many times they've bought, which decides what they need to hear next. Build the engaged segment first, then these on top of it.

What should I send lapsed customers to bring them back?

Lead with what's new or different, not a discount. A lapsed buyer knows the product and chose to stop, and a discount tells them it's worth less now. Novelty gives them a reason to look again. An offer can come later in a win-back; it shouldn't be the opener.
