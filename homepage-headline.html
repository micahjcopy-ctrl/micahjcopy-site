Your best homepage headline is already written, by your customers.

You've been staring at the headline field for a week, hunting for the clever line that sums up the business. Stop hunting. The line that sells isn't clever, and you're not the one who writes it. Your customers already did, in their reviews, on sales calls, and in the emails they send when something goes wrong.

TAGLINES DON'T SELL THE WORDS ARE IN REVIEWS MODEST BEATS BIG

"Reclaim Your Health." Then: "Finally, a guided process for women struggling with fatigue and bloating." Same business, two headlines. The first is what most owners write and most designers approve, because it sounds like a brand. The second is the one that sells, because it says who it's for and what the problem is, in the words the customer herself would use. The gap between the two isn't cleverness. It's abstraction, and every point of it costs you a visitor. Your customers have already closed that gap in writing.

The test a headline has to pass

If the headline stood completely alone, with no page under it, would it sell the offer? That's the test, and "Reclaim Your Health" fails it on contact. Alone, it could be a gym, a supplement, a clinic, a juice bar. It tells the reader nothing about whether this is for them. "Finally, a guided process for women struggling with fatigue and bloating" passes. A woman with fatigue and bloating knows in a glance that this is for her, that someone will walk her through it, and that she can stop looking. Most headlines fail the test the same way, by being too abstract or too vague to sell anything on their own.

The tagline instinct comes from a reasonable place. The owner wants something memorable and brand-shaped, and the designer wants something short enough to look good in the mockup. But a stranger isn't trying to remember you. They're looking for the answer to "is this my problem, and does this fix it," and the deeper they have to think to get that answer, the more of them stop thinking and leave. When the copy mirrors the reader's own experience in their own language, they nod, and a reader who's nodding keeps reading. Which leaves one question: where does that language come from? Not from a brainstorm.

How to write a website headline: don't write it, find it

It comes from the people who already bought. Three places hold it. Your reviews, where customers describe the problem they had and the result they got with no marketing in the way. Your sales-call notes, where prospects say what they tried before and why they're on the phone now. And your support emails, where people tell you what they were hoping for in the plainest terms they have. Read through all three looking for phrases that repeat. The ones that keep showing up are the ones that connected, and a headline built from them attracts more of the same kind of buyer, because it's written in that buyer's voice. If you run ads, there's a fourth place: the ad that gets the cheapest clicks and the most sales is telling you how the audience wants the thing described, and its opening line belongs on the page, so the headline pays off what the ad promised.

I studied a case that shows how much is sitting there. A brand's page had a line deep in the copy, the insight buyers kept responding to, mined from testimonials, sales calls, support groups and reviews. The team moved that line up into the headline and tested it. Add-to-cart went from ten percent of visitors to fifteen, and conversions rose by about a tenth, from the headline test alone. The winning line took a direct stance against the usual solutions and leaned on real proof and an expert's authority. Nobody invented it. They found it on the page and in the customers' mouths, and gave it the top spot. The words you need are probably sitting in your inbox right now. The next decision is how big to make them.

The rule

A headline's job is to say what the customer gets, in the words the customer already uses for the problem. If it could sit on a competitor's site, or on a different business entirely, it isn't a headline yet. It's a tagline.

Say less than you could

Once you've got the customer's words, the temptation is to improve them. Make the number bigger, the timeline shorter, the promise louder. Sometimes that works. But in a market where every competitor already shouts, the loud claim is wallpaper, and a modest, grounded one cuts through because it's the first thing the reader has believed all day. "An extra $2,000 a month" lands with a jaded audience where "six figures tomorrow" doesn't. Your customers' own phrases are usually modest, because people describe real results modestly. Keep them that way.

The same restraint fixes product pages. A page headed "HydroSkin Serum" is a label, not a headline. "HydroSkin Serum: instantly hydrates and firms in 60 seconds" keeps the name and adds the outcome, and the outcome is the part a buyer came looking for. Keep the name, state what it does, and don't reach past what a customer has actually said about it. A modest promise has one more advantage, and it's the one that matters next: it's a promise the rest of the page can keep.

Change the headline, then read what's under it

A headline like that, modest and in the customer's words, is also the fastest change you can make to a site. No design work, no developer, often no cost, and every visitor sees it. That's exactly why it's dangerous to do alone. A headline that promises something the next section doesn't continue has made a promise the page breaks. The same kind of test has gone the other way: a new headline lifted add-to-carts, and then drop-offs appeared further down the page, because the order of what came after no longer matched what the top had set up. The headline won. The page lost. A headline is the first promise on the page, and the sections after it are how the promise gets kept, in order.

So after you swap it, read the first section below the fold the way a stranger would and ask whether it picks up the headline's story or starts a new one. Then step back and look at the whole first screen as one space, the headline, the line under it, the proof and the button, and check it does what the top of a page is for. Then run the five-second test on it with someone who doesn't know your business. If they repeat your customers' words back to you, you've got the headline. It was there the whole time.

Finding the headline in the customer's words is where every site I write starts, before any design, because the design has to be built around it. If you'd rather have me go through your reviews and call notes and pull it out, that's the positioning work at the front of my website design process, and it's the part that decides whether the rest of the site has anything to say.

Common questions

What makes a good website headline?

It says what the customer gets, for whom, in the words the customer uses for the problem. The test: if it stood alone with no page under it, would it sell the offer? Most fail by being abstract. Reclaim Your Health could be anything; a guided process for women with bloating is for one person.

Where do I find the words for my headline?

In your reviews, your sales-call notes and your support emails. Look for phrases that repeat; those are the ones that connected. A headline built from them attracts more of the same kind of buyer, and it's usually more modest than the one you'd write, which is a strength.

Should my headline make a bigger promise?

Usually not. In markets where every competitor shouts, a modest, grounded claim cuts through and gets believed. Keep the customer's own phrasing, state the outcome plainly, and make sure the section under the headline continues the same story so the page keeps the promise.
